Results
Hardest problem

Problem

Covering 600 MHz with one varactor would need a high KVCO, which turns control-line noise into phase noise.

What we did

Split the range into 8 overlapping bands with a 3-bit switched-capacitor bank (my block) and left only fine tuning to the varactor.

Result

Gap-free 6.71–7.30 GHz coverage with peak |KVCO| of 160–190 MHz/V against a 500 MHz/V limit, and −111 dBc/Hz worst case.

Roles

I designed the switched-capacitor tuning bank and its bias, modeled the spiral inductor in ASITIC, and extracted the performance data. My teammate designed the cross-coupled core and varactor path and set up the PSS, harmonic-balance and phase-noise simulations.

What the data showed

  • The eight bands tune monotonically and overlap, so 6.71–7.30 GHz is covered without gaps.
  • About 80% of the phase noise comes from the cross-coupled pair and about 10% from the tail source.
  • The ASITIC model brought the tank's real series resistance and Q into the phase-noise result.

30 GHz power dividers and branch-line hybrid coupler

T-junction, Wilkinson and 90° branch-line designs at 30 GHz on an εr = 2.1 substrate, built from λ/4 = 1.73 mm lines and simulated from 20 to 40 GHz in Keysight ADS.

Results
Hardest problem

Problem

The T-junction split power evenly but its outputs were only about 6 dB isolated, and one output port was badly matched.

What I did

Moved to a Wilkinson with a 2·Z0 resistor between the outputs, so the odd-mode signal is absorbed instead of passed between ports.

Result

Output isolation went from about 6 dB to 55.5 dB, with every port matched better than −56 dB at 30 GHz.

What the comparison showed

  • The T-junction splits power but its outputs see each other directly: S23 ≈ −6 dB and an unmatched output port (S33 ≈ −6 dB).
  • Adding the 100 Ω resistor between the Wilkinson arms absorbs the odd-mode signal, which takes output isolation from ~6 dB to 55 dB and matches every port.
  • The branch-line hybrid gives an equal split with the outputs 90° apart, the building block for balanced amplifiers and I/Q mixers.

Wideband extension

I also tried a two-section stepped-impedance Wilkinson for 24–36 GHz. It held the split to −3.26 to −3.36 dB across the band, but with a single isolation resistor the isolation was only about 4 dB at 30 GHz. A multi-section Wilkinson needs a resistor at every section, which is the fix for the next revision.

Results
Hardest problem

Problem

Gain stayed about 8.5 dB below the 89 dB target.

What we did

Traced it to the resistive CMFB sense network loading the first-stage output and to tight headroom in the bias. We tried NMOS and PMOS gain-boosting amplifiers, but they pushed the cascodes out of saturation.

Result

Kept a stable design at 301 MHz GBW and 79.2° phase margin, and identified the fix: a higher-headroom bias and a cascoded second stage.

Roles

I designed the bias network: a bootstrapped current reference and wide-swing mirrors for every cascode bias. My teammate designed the second stage and handled stability. The folded-cascode core was joint work.

Trade-offs and lessons

  • Gain: the resistive CMFB sense network loaded the first-stage output, the main reason gain landed below 89 dB.
  • Swing: the simple bias left little headroom across the folded devices.
  • Slew and settling: pushing GBW to 301 MHz and PM to 79° needed a compensation cap the available current could not slew quickly.
  • Next time: a higher-headroom bias, a cascoded or gain-boosted second stage, and GBW/PM set just above spec.

Hardest problem

Problem

The high-side GaN driver floats on a switch node slewing at about 14.7 V/ns, where false triggering is a real risk, and the digital isolator had no SPICE model.

What I did

Characterized gate drive, dead time and switch-node edges over four cycles, using transformer isolation in place of the missing isolator model.

Result

Clean 0–5 V gate drive with no shoot-through overlap, 3.26 ns / 5.65 ns edges, and 180° interleaving confirmed.

Roles

I did the electrical characterization: gate drive, dead time, the bootstrapped high-side driver, 180° interleaving and the switch-node edges in the table.

Team results

  • 93.7% simulated efficiency at full load including layout parasitics.
  • GaN junctions around 77 °C in Ansys Icepak with 25 W total heat (teammate).
  • 18-element Q3D parasitic model reduced to 7 elements for common-mode analysis, plus a shield layer for common-mode current (teammates).
  • The CM estimate exceeded CISPR Class A from 200 kHz to 5 MHz; a CM choke and larger shield were the next steps.

65–85 V in to 25 V/150 W, 50 V/100 W and 150 V/300 W isolated outputs at 50 kHz, with a closed voltage loop in PLECS.

Hardest problem

Problem

The first design lost 17.9 W in the switch, rectifiers and capacitors, too close to the 92% efficiency floor.

What I did

Rebuilt the loss budget device by device, swapped the 55 mΩ MOSFET for an 18 mΩ part, and moved to Schottky and ultrafast rectifiers.

Result

Loss fell to 14.1 W and estimated efficiency to about 93.8%, clearing the target.

Design decisions

  • Turns ratios set at 65 V with Dmax = 0.45 (reflected voltage ≈ 53 V); duty falls to 0.385 at 85 V.
  • Lm ≈ 160 µH keeps the converter in CCM at full load.
  • Swapped the 55 mΩ FDPF24N25 for an 18 mΩ STP25N018M9 and moved to Schottky/ultrafast rectifiers.
  • The loop regulates the 25 V output; the 50 V and 150 V outputs are cross-regulated, and the response is overdamped (~65 ms rise). Next: higher crossover and weighted multi-output feedback.

Hardest problem

Problem

During writes, unselected columns wrote floating bit-line values into the array, and a timing mismatch between wordline and address edges caused a glitch.

What we did

AND-gated each write wordline with the column-address bit so only the selected columns fire, and added two inverters of delay on one gate input.

Result

Correct write behavior at a 175 ps clock with an LVS/DRC-clean top level.

Roles: I designed the 3-to-8 read and write row decoders (schematic and layout) and shared LVS/DRC debugging and top-level integration with my two teammates.

Contactless laser vibration monitoring on motors

A condition-monitoring system using laser-reflection vibration sensors on more than 100 motors at an automotive plant. I designed the power and signal-conditioning board for the sensor nodes and supported the rollout on site. Each sensor streams target velocity and distance at 6 or 12 kHz in 600-sample packets, which the ESP32 firmware publishes as JSON over MQTT or passes over serial to a bridge that writes RMS values into Modbus TCP registers for the plant PLC.

Bearing defect frequencies used to read the vibration spectra. fr is shaft speed, N the number of rolling elements, d the ball diameter, D the pitch diameter and φ the contact angle.

FTF (cage)
(fr/2) · (1 − (d/D)·cos φ)
BPFO
(N·fr/2) · (1 − (d/D)·cos φ)
BPFI
(N·fr/2) · (1 + (d/D)·cos φ)
BSF
(D/2d) · fr · (1 − ((d/D)·cos φ)²)

A rise at one of these lines, or at its harmonics and sidebands, points to the outer race, inner race, rolling elements or cage.
